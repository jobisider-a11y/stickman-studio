Inside the Black Box

June first, two thousand nine. It's the middle of the night over the Atlantic Ocean. An Airbus A three-thirty is flying from Rio de Janeiro to Paris, with two hundred and twenty-eight people on board.

Somewhere far from any coast, the plane flies into a region of thunderstorms. And then, it simply stops reporting. No mayday. No warning call. Nothing.

Search teams find wreckage on the surface within days. But the most important question has no answer. A modern jet, flown by an experienced crew, fell out of the sky. Why?

The answer is lying almost four kilometers below the surface, in two small orange boxes. And it will take almost two years to reach them.

Stay with me, because by the end of this video, you'll know exactly what those boxes revealed, and how one tiny frozen sensor started it all.

This is the story of the black box.

First, let's clear something up. The black box isn't black. It's bright orange, often with reflective stripes, so searchers can spot it in wreckage, in a forest, or on the bottom of the ocean.

So why the name? Nobody knows for sure. Some say the very first versions were painted black. Some say "black box" was simply old slang for any mysterious electronic gadget. Others say it's because the boxes were found scorched and charred after a fire. Take your pick.

And here's the second surprise. It isn't one box. It's two.

The first is the cockpit voice recorder. It records everything the pilots say, every radio call, every alarm, every click of a switch, through microphones in their headsets and one in the cockpit itself.

The second is the flight data recorder. It quietly logs the plane's measurements, many times every second: speed, altitude, engine power, the position of every control. Modern recorders can track well over a thousand of them.

Think of it like this. Every second of every flight becomes a long row of numbers. String those rows together, and you get a perfect digital record of everything the plane did, from push back to parking.

After an accident, investigators feed those numbers into a computer and rebuild the flight as an animation, matched to the sounds from the cockpit. They can watch exactly where the plane went, what the controls did, and what the crew heard, moment by moment.

That work is done by independent investigators, like the NTSB in the United States, the BEA in France, or the AAIB in the U.K. And their job isn't to blame anyone. It's to find out what happened, so it can't happen again.

Together, they can rebuild the final minutes of a flight almost second by second.

Quick question before we go on. Where on the plane do you think the recorders are kept? The nose, the middle, or the tail? Keep your answer in mind. We'll find out in a few minutes. But someone had to invent them first. And it started with a boy who lost his father.

Nineteen thirty-four. In Australia, a young boy named David Warren loses his father in a plane crash over the sea. Nobody ever finds out exactly what happened.

Almost twenty years later, Warren is a scientist at an aeronautical research lab in Melbourne. And the aviation world has a terrifying new mystery: the de Havilland Comet, the world's first jet airliner, is breaking apart in mid-air.

The cause turned out to be metal fatigue. Every flight, the cabin was pressurized and then released, flexing the metal again and again. And the sharp corners around some openings concentrated that stress until cracks spread. It's a big part of why airliner windows today have rounded corners.

Warren is asked to help think about why. And sitting in that meeting, he has a simple idea. What if the cockpit had a recorder? What if investigators could hear the last words of the crew, and know exactly what the instruments were saying?

At the time, there were already some flight recorders, but they captured only a few basic measurements. Warren wanted something that would capture the voices, too.

By the late nineteen fifties, he had built a working prototype. It could record hours of cockpit sound and instrument readings, and it was small enough to fit in a plane.

It stored about four hours of sound and instrument data on a spool of thin steel wire, recording over itself again and again, so it always held the most recent hours.

And almost nobody wanted it. Some officials couldn't see the point. And pilots hated the idea of a microphone recording everything they said. One complaint stuck: it was like having a spy in the cockpit.

Warren didn't give up. Interest grew in Britain, where an early commercial version was built. Then, after a deadly crash in Australia in nineteen sixty left investigators with no answers, Australia became one of the first countries to require cockpit recorders on airliners.

Over the following decades, the rest of the world followed. Today, every large airliner carries both recorders.

And the pilots' worry about a spy in the cockpit was taken seriously. In many countries, cockpit voice recordings are legally protected. They're used for safety investigations, not to listen in on pilots, and the actual audio is very rarely made public.

The boy who never learned why his father's plane went down ended up making sure that, for countless families after him, there would usually be an answer.

But a recorder is useless if it doesn't survive the crash. So how do you build a box that can't be destroyed?

Here's the trick. Most of a black box is protection. The part that actually matters, the memory, is a small, heavily armored core in the middle, wrapped in layers of insulation and steel or titanium.

And before a design is approved, it's put through tests that sound almost cruel. Test one: impact. The box is fired at a target so it stops with a force of about three thousand four hundred times gravity.

Test two: a heavy steel spike is dropped onto it, to make sure nothing sharp in a crash can punch through.

Test three: it's crushed under a press with tons of force, for minutes at a time.

Test four: fire. Jet fuel burns hot, so the box is blasted with flames of more than a thousand degrees Celsius for up to an hour.

Test five: the deep ocean. It's held under pressure like the bottom of the sea, around six kilometers down, for a month, to prove the water and the pressure can't get in.

And there's more. It's soaked in jet fuel, hydraulic fluid, and fire-fighting chemicals, all the nasty liquids that can end up around a crash site, to make sure none of them can eat their way inside.

Only if the memory inside still reads perfectly afterwards does the design pass.

And remember my question? Even where they sit is part of the plan. The recorders are usually mounted near the tail, because in many crashes the back of the aircraft takes less of the impact than the front.

Inside, the technology changed too. Early recorders used metal foil and magnetic tape, with moving parts that could break. Modern ones store everything on solid-state memory chips, like a much tougher version of the memory in your phone.

And even when the outside of a box is badly damaged, investigators can often remove the memory board, clean it, and read it directly in the lab. The armor only has to protect a tiny thing.

For years, the cockpit voice recorder kept only the last two hours of sound, recording over the oldest audio again and again. Newer rules are pushing that to twenty-five hours, so investigators don't lose anything important that happened earlier in a long flight.

Another lesson came from accidents where the plane's electrical power failed, and the recorders went silent at exactly the wrong moment. Many newer cockpit recorders now carry their own small backup power supply, so they keep recording for around ten minutes after the plane's power is lost.

There's one more piece. Bolted to each recorder is a small beacon. If it lands in water, it switches on and starts sending out a high-pitched ping, once every second, so search teams can hear it.

But that beacon runs on a battery. And for one flight, that battery ran out long before anyone found it.

Back to June two thousand nine. Ships race to the area where the plane went down, towing sensitive underwater microphones, listening for the pings.

The problem is the ocean itself. The search area is huge, and the sea floor there is a landscape of underwater mountains and canyons, thousands of meters deep.

Searching it is like mowing a lawn in the dark. Ships tow sonar back and forth in long stripes, slowly painting a picture of the sea floor, strip by strip, looking for anything that doesn't belong.

The beacons are designed to ping for about thirty days. The searchers listen for weeks. They never hear them. And then the batteries go silent.

Search after search comes up empty. Months pass. Then a whole year. Then almost two.

In two thousand eleven, a new search uses underwater robots, torpedo-shaped vehicles that glide just above the sea floor, scanning it with sonar, mile after mile.

The robots used in that search came from the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution in the United States, and they worked in shifts, diving for hours, surfacing, and diving again.

And finally, in April, almost two years after the crash, they find the debris field. Nearly four kilometers down.

A remotely operated vehicle goes down, and lifts the recorders out of the mud.

After almost two years under crushing pressure, in the cold and the dark, the memory chips are carefully cleaned, dried, and read. And the data is there. Almost all of it.

There's a clever trick to that. Boxes recovered from the sea are often kept in water until they reach the lab, because drying out too fast can let salt and corrosion damage the electronics.

Then comes the decoding. The raw data is just a stream of ones and zeros in the manufacturer's own format. Investigators translate it, check it against the plane's design, and turn it back into the flight.

Not every search ends like this. In two thousand fourteen, Malaysia Airlines flight three seventy disappeared with two hundred and thirty-nine people on board. Searchers combed a vast stretch of the Indian Ocean. Pieces of the plane eventually washed up on distant shores. But its recorders have never been found. And that changed the rules.

Newer beacons now ping for ninety days instead of thirty. Airliners are being required to report their position more often over oceans. And engineers are working on recorders that eject and float, and systems that can stream key flight data live, before a plane ever hits the water.

But in two thousand eleven, in a lab in France, investigators finally pressed play. And for the first time, they heard what really happened that night.

The recordings start calmly. The plane is cruising at night, at around thirty-five thousand feet, approaching an area of storms near the equator.

Remember the pitot tubes from our last video, those little tubes on the nose that measure airspeed? Flying through the storm clouds, they begin to clog with ice crystals.

Within seconds, the airspeed readings become unreliable. The plane no longer knows how fast it's going.

Without a trustworthy airspeed, the autopilot does exactly what it's designed to do. It disconnects, and hands the plane back to the pilots, suddenly, in the dark, in turbulence.

What happened next is the hardest part. The data shows the pilot flying pulled the nose up, and the plane climbed. As it climbed, it slowed. And the wings, losing the airflow they needed, stopped producing enough lift.

That's called a stall. Not an engine stall. A wing stall. The airflow over the wing breaks away, and the wing stops holding the plane up.

The stall warning sounded again and again. But in the confusion, with conflicting readings and alarms, the crew never fully recognized what was happening.

And the recordings revealed two details that made it even harder. At times, the airspeed readings dropped so low that the system treated them as invalid, and the stall warning actually went quiet. Then, when the nose came down a little, the readings returned and the warning started again, sending exactly the wrong signal.

On this aircraft, the two pilots' side sticks aren't mechanically linked. So one pilot couldn't feel what the other was doing with his controls. For most of the descent, the plane's nose stayed up.

The way out of a stall is counterintuitive. You lower the nose, let the plane gain speed, and let the air flow smoothly over the wings again. Pulling up only makes it worse.

The plane fell for about three and a half minutes, nose high, belly first, until it hit the ocean. Everyone on board was lost.

Two hundred and twenty-eight people. And for almost two years, nobody knew why.

Because the boxes survived, the investigators could answer that question, and change things. Airlines replaced that model of pitot tube. Pilots around the world began training much more on what to do when airspeed readings fail, and on recovering from a stall at high altitude. Cockpit procedures and warnings were rethought.

And that's the real job of the black box. It isn't to tell a sad story. It's to make sure the same story never happens again. Many of the rules, checklists, and safety features on your flight today exist because a recorder survived and told investigators the truth.

It's a big part of why flying has become one of the safest ways to travel, even as more people fly than ever.

So the next time you board a plane, remember: somewhere near the tail, two orange boxes are already listening. Not to spy. But to make sure that, whatever happens, the next flight is safer than the last.

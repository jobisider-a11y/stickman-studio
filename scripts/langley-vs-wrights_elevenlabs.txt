December eighth, nineteen-oh-three. A freezing river in Washington, D.C. A giant flying machine sits on a catapult, on top of a houseboat.

<break time="0.6s" />

Its creator is Samuel Langley, head of the Smithsonian. The U.S. Army has given him fifty thousand dollars. The whole country expects him to build the first airplane.

The catapult fires. <break time="0.7s" /> The machine snaps apart and plunges straight into the Potomac. Its pilot is dragged from the icy water, lucky to be alive.

It was Langley's second crash in two months. After the first one, the New York Times predicted a real flying machine might take one to ten million years.

<break time="0.6s" /> It took nine days. <break time="0.8s" />

On December seventeenth, at Kitty Hawk, two bicycle mechanics from Dayton, Ohio, flew for twelve seconds. Orville and Wilbur Wright. No government money. By most accounts, they spent less than a thousand dollars.

But the story didn't end there. Years later, the Smithsonian modified Langley's machine, flew it, and claimed it could have flown first. <break time="0.4s" /> The Wrights were furious. Orville sent their original airplane to a museum in London. It didn't come home to America until nineteen forty-eight.

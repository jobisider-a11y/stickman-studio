Right now, outside the window of almost every airliner in the sky, the air is around minus fifty-five degrees Celsius. It's so thin that without oxygen, you'd have well under a minute of useful consciousness. And yet, this is exactly where pilots want to be.

Thirty-five thousand feet. About ten and a half kilometers up. Higher than Mount Everest, by more than a mile. It's the number your captain announces on flight after flight. But why there? Why not lower, where the air is warm and thick? Or higher, where it's even emptier? The answer is a balancing act. And once you see it, you'll never hear that announcement the same way again.

First: thin air is cheap air. Flying through air is a bit like wading through water. Near the ground, it's thick, and it pushes back hard. At thirty-five thousand feet, the air is only about a third as dense as at sea level. Less air pushing back means less drag. Less drag means less fuel. And for an airline, fuel is one of the biggest bills there is.

So why not just keep climbing forever? Because the air that slows you down is also the air that holds you up.

Wings make lift by pushing air downward. Engines need air to burn their fuel. Thin it out too much, and the plane has to fly faster and faster just to stay in the sky. Climb high enough, and the slowest speed the plane can safely fly and the fastest speed it's allowed to fly start squeezing together. Pilots have a grim name for that narrow gap. The coffin corner.

So the plane hunts for a sweet spot. And up there, there's a bonus.

Most of our weather lives in the lowest layer of the atmosphere, the troposphere. Its ceiling sits at roughly thirty-six thousand feet in the mid-latitudes. Cruise near that ceiling, and you're above much of the rain, cloud, and chaos below. Not all of it. The biggest thunderstorms punch even higher, which is why pilots still steer around them.

Next, the wind. Up here flow the jet streams, rivers of air that can race along at well over a hundred miles an hour. Ride one, and you arrive early on less fuel. That's a big reason a flight from New York to London is often about an hour shorter than the trip back.

And then, there's traffic. In much of the world, eastbound flights cruise at odd thousands, like thirty-five thousand, and westbound flights at even ones, like thirty-six thousand. Thousands of aircraft, stacked in invisible lanes.

But here's the twist. Thirty-five thousand isn't a magic number. It's a moving target. A long-haul jet can take off carrying more than a hundred tons of fuel. That makes it too heavy for its best altitude. So it starts lower. And as it burns fuel and gets lighter, it climbs, step by step. Your plane literally rises higher as the flight goes on.

So the next time your captain says, "We're cruising at thirty-five thousand feet," you'll know that number wasn't random. It was worked out for your plane's weight, today's winds, and the traffic around you. A quiet calculation, made in freezing, thin air, more than a mile above the top of Everest.
